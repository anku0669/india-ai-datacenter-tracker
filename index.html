India's AI Data Center Boom — Tracked Live

India is in the middle of the largest data-center build-out in its history. Hyperscalers, conglomerates and state governments have announced well over US $150 billion in new AI infrastructure since 2025 — from Reliance's 1.5 GW cluster in Visakhapatnam to Adani's US $100 billion, 2035 roadmap. This page tracks where that capacity is landing, how much power and water it consumes against India's total national resources, what happens if the country keeps building, and who bears the cost.

Inspired by and modeled after the U.S. community-awareness project Brockovich Data Center Reporting, which maps AI data centers and citizen concerns across the United States. This is an independent, India-focused adaptation built from public data — not affiliated with that project.

About This Tracker

This page is an independent, open-source effort to do for India what the Brockovich Data Center Reporting project has done for the United States: put the scattered, hard-to-find facts about AI infrastructure — who is building what, where, how big, and at what environmental cost — onto a single, continuously updatable page that any citizen, journalist, policymaker or investor can check. It is not affiliated with any government body, operator, or the original Brockovich project, and it does not represent itself as an official regulator's dataset.

Methodology: every figure on this page is sourced from public disclosures — company press releases, stock-exchange filings, sector reports from KPMG and SBI, Ministry of Power / CEA data, Central Water Commission assessments, state government incentive notifications, and investigative journalism from outlets including Down To Earth, Eco-Business, Scroll.in and The Hindu BusinessLine. Where sources disagree (which happens often, especially on water use), this page states the range and cites both figures rather than picking one. The dataset is maintained as plain, human-readable code (data.js) on GitHub, so anyone can audit, fork, or submit a correction.

⚡ India's Total Power Generation Capacity

Before judging whether data centers are a big deal for India's grid, here is the full national picture: how much power India generates today, from what sources, and how a fast-growing AI data-center sector compares against that total.

As of March 2026, India's total installed power generation capacity is 533 GW, built from coal (229 GW, 43%), solar (150 GW, 28%), wind (56 GW, 11%), large hydro (51 GW, 10%), nuclear (9 GW, 2%) and smaller sources like bio-energy and small hydro making up the rest. The country generated ~1,846 TWh (billion units) of electricity in FY 2025-26.

Non-fossil sources crossed 50% of installed capacity in June 2025, years ahead of India's 2030 NDC climate target — a genuinely fast clean-energy transition. The Ministry of Power's National Electricity Plan projects total capacity reaching 874 GW by 2031-32 and 1,121 GW by 2035-36, with non-fossil sources expected to reach ~70% of that mix.

Against this backdrop, India's data centers currently draw about 1.9 GW — just 0.36% of national capacity. Even the full 12 GW pipeline projected for 2030 would be only around 1.4% of India's projected 2031-32 grid. Nationally, this is a small slice. The real question — covered in the sections below — is not the national percentage but where that new load lands, how fast it arrives relative to grid upgrades, and whether it is matched with genuinely additional renewable capacity rather than diverting clean power away from other users.

💧 India's Total Water Resources

The same national-vs-local logic applies to water. Here is how much water India actually has, how much of it is usable, and how that compares to what data centers draw today and could draw by 2030.

India's total average annual water resources are about 2,116 billion cubic meters (BCM), per the Central Water Commission's 2024 assessment (updated from an earlier 1,999 BCM estimate, largely by including more trans-boundary river-basin water). Of that, only around 1,123 BCM is realistically utilizable — 690 BCM from surface water and 433 BCM from replenishable groundwater — because the rest is lost to evaporation, flows to the sea, or is inaccessible.

Because most of India's rain falls in a three-month monsoon window and population has grown steadily, per-capita water availability has fallen to about 1,513 cubic meters per year — below the internationally recognized "water-stressed" threshold of 1,700 m³ (and edging toward the 1,000 m³ "water-scarce" line). Agriculture alone consumes roughly 80% of India's total water use, dwarfing every other sector combined.

Data centers, by contrast, are estimated to use about 150 billion litres (0.15 BCM) nationally in 2025 — roughly 0.007% of India's total water resources, rising to an estimated 358 billion litres (~0.36 BCM, ~0.017%) by 2030. At the national level this is a rounding error. The problem, again, is concentration: most of that draw lands in a handful of already-stressed river basins and cities (Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Chennai, Thane), not spread evenly across the country's water surplus regions like the Brahmaputra or Ganga basins.

🗓️ Timeline of Major Announcements (2025-2026)

December 2025 — AWS pledges an additional $7 billion for Hyderabad; Microsoft announces a $17.5 billion AI infrastructure investment spanning Hyderabad and Pune. Within weeks, the scale of committed hyperscaler capital in India roughly doubles.

Early-to-mid 2026 — AWS discloses a $430 million data center near Navi Mumbai (Taloja) and pledges a further $13 billion through 2030, bringing its cumulative India commitment to roughly $48 billion. Google announces a $15 billion, 1 GW campus in Visakhapatnam alongside three new subsea cable systems.

June 2026 — Reliance Industries and Meta announce a 168 MW, build-to-suit AI data center in Jamnagar, Gujarat — Meta's first dedicated facility in India, notable for its desalinated-seawater cooling design.

Mid-2026 — Reliance unveils its flagship 1.5 GW, three-phase Visakhapatnam cluster (~$17-19 billion), positioning it as potentially India's single largest data center investment, with Phase 1 targeted for commercial operation by October 2028.

August 2026 — Adani Group discloses plans for a $100 billion, decade-long (through 2035) roadmap to scale AdaniConneX from 2 GW to roughly 5 GW of national capacity, alongside a proposed ₹1 trillion, 1 GW facility in Odisha and continuing partnerships with Google and Microsoft. In the same window, community pushback surfaces publicly in Thane over Amazon's Balkum facility, and legal and civil-society groups intensify calls to suspend approvals for undisclosed water sourcing in the Vizag cluster.

⚡ Data Center Power Consumption, In Detail

Zooming in from the national picture above to how data centers specifically consume and source power.

India's installed data center capacity has grown from roughly 778 MW in FY23 to ~1.9 GW in FY26 — more than doubling in three years. Industry trackers now put the near-term pipeline at a further 4.5 GW over the next five years, with long-range projections from Adani and independent analysts pointing to ~12 GW by 2030, roughly a 6x increase from today.

For scale: a single 1 GW AI campus (like the ones planned by Reliance and Google in Visakhapatnam) draws roughly as much continuous power as a mid-sized Indian city. Multiply that across the more than a dozen gigawatt-scale projects now announced — Adani (2 GW → 5 GW), Reliance (1.5 GW Vizag + 168 MW Jamnagar), Google (1 GW), Microsoft ($17.5B across Hyderabad/Pune) and AWS ($48B cumulative across Mumbai/Hyderabad/ Navi Mumbai) — and the scale of new demand on India's grid becomes clear.

On efficiency, the picture is more positive: top Indian operators report Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) below 1.5, in line with global best practice, and roughly 50% of installed capacity is now paired with renewable power — solar, wind or captive renewable-plus-storage setups, as in Reliance's Vizag cluster and Adani's integrated energy-and-compute platform. Even so, 25-30% of existing capacity is considered under-specified for dense AI/GPU workloads and will need power and cooling retrofits.

About 90% of current capacity is concentrated in just four metros — Mumbai, Chennai, Delhi NCR and Bengaluru — putting concentrated new load on regional grids that were not originally sized for gigawatt-scale, 24/7 industrial compute demand. Hyderabad, Pune and Visakhapatnam are the fastest-growing "Tier II" hubs absorbing the overflow.

Unlike traditional industrial loads that fluctuate with shifts and seasons, AI training and inference clusters draw power around the clock at near-constant utilization, which is precisely why hyperscalers are pairing new campuses with dedicated generation rather than relying solely on the public grid. Reliance's Vizag cluster and Adani's platform both bundle captive solar, wind and battery storage directly into the data center build — in effect, India's AI boom is quietly becoming one of the country's largest private drivers of new renewable-plus-storage capacity, independent of any AI-specific policy mandate.

💧 Data Center Water Consumption, In Detail

The least-disclosed number in the entire sector — zooming in on how, where, and how much data centers actually draw.

National data center water-use estimates. 2025 figure and 2030 projection per Eco-Business reporting; interim years interpolated to match the capacity growth curve.

Most Indian data centers still rely on evaporative cooling, which is cheap and energy-efficient but consumes large volumes of fresh water. One widely cited estimate from Karnataka puts consumption at roughly 25 million litres per MW per year. Applied to India's ~1.5-1.9 GW of live capacity, that alone implies tens of billions of litres a year before the current expansion wave is even counted.

The geography is the real concern: an estimated 75% of India's data centers sit in water-stressed regions, and a WRI India mapping exercise found more than half of facilities in areas already competing for scarce municipal and agricultural water. Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Chennai — three of India's top data-center hubs — are also three cities that regularly face acute water shortages.

Transparency is a persistent problem. A Down To Earth investigation of six major operators — Nxtra by Airtel, AdaniConneX, STT GDC India, NTT, CtrlS and Sify — found inconsistent, non-comparable ESG water disclosures with major year-to-year gaps: STT GDC reported total withdrawals rising to 1,149,020 KL in 2024; NTT disclosed 413,779 KL in 2023 but omitted water data in its 2025 report entirely; AdaniConneX has no standalone water disclosure at all. In Thane, residents found three contradictory official figures for the same Amazon facility — 10 KLD, 125 KLD and a planned 12 MLD municipal allocation — illustrating how hard it is for the public to verify real consumption.

Cooling technology choice matters enormously here. Evaporative and open-loop chilled-water systems lose most of their water to the atmosphere as vapor and cannot be meaningfully recycled on-site; closed-loop liquid cooling and air-based free cooling use far less water but typically cost more to build and, in hot climates, deliver a smaller efficiency benefit. Reliance's move to desalinated seawater at Jamnagar, and its planned desalination plant for the Vizag cluster, are among the first large-scale attempts in India to decouple gigawatt-scale AI cooling from freshwater and groundwater altogether — a model worth tracking as a possible national standard.

🌎 Environmental Concerns Beyond Power & Water

Power and water get most of the attention, but a gigawatt-scale build-out touches land, air, biodiversity and the waste stream too. Each concern below is distinct enough to warrant its own scrutiny.

🌫️

Carbon emissions tied to the grid mix

Coal still supplies 43% of India's installed power capacity. A data center plugged into the public grid without a dedicated renewable-plus-storage contract inherits that carbon intensity. Even with ~50% non-fossil capacity nationally, actual generation still leans on thermal plants during peak and non-solar hours — exactly when 24/7 AI compute needs power most.

🔋

Diesel backup generators: air & noise pollution

Nearly all large data centers run diesel generators for backup power. These emit particulate matter and NOx during testing and outages, and constant cooling-system and generator noise has been a recurring nuisance complaint near U.S. facilities — a pattern regulators expect to repeat in dense Indian metros as campuses scale up.

🏗️

Land use, deforestation & local ecosystems

Individual campuses run 250-935+ acres (Odisha's proposed Adani site, Reliance's Vizag land allocation). At gigawatt scale this means significant conversion of agricultural, coastal or forested land, with associated topsoil loss, habitat fragmentation, and reduced natural groundwater recharge from paved, impervious surfaces.

🐟

Coastal & marine impact from desalination

Desalinated seawater cooling (Jamnagar, planned for Vizag) solves the freshwater problem but creates a new one: concentrated brine discharge back into coastal waters can raise local salinity and harm marine ecosystems if not carefully managed and monitored — a trade-off regulators have not yet built clear rules around.

♻️

E-waste from rapid hardware refresh cycles

AI-optimized GPU hardware is refreshed far more frequently than traditional enterprise IT — often every 2-3 years versus 5-7. A sector scaling from 1.9 GW to 12+ GW implies a proportional surge in decommissioned servers, GPUs and networking gear, and India currently lacks data-center-specific e-waste recycling mandates.

🌡️

Localized heat island effects

Dense clusters of always-on cooling equipment raise ambient temperature in their immediate vicinity, compounding urban heat-island effects already present in metros like Mumbai, Chennai and Hyderabad — a second-order climate burden that falls on nearby residents, not the operator.

✅ Why This Build-Out Is Good for India

The upside case, broken into its distinct parts.

🛡️

Digital sovereignty & strategic autonomy

Hosting AI compute domestically means Indian companies, startups and government agencies can run sensitive workloads — health records, financial data, defense-adjacent research — without depending entirely on foreign infrastructure. Adani's platform explicitly reserves GPU capacity for Indian startups, research institutions and deep-tech ventures, framing the effort as part of a national "five-layer AI stack" (applications, models, chips, energy, data centers).

💰

Direct and induced investment

The announced pipeline — Adani's $100B, AWS's cumulative $48B, Microsoft's $17.5B, Google's $15B, and Reliance's roughly $17B+ Vizag cluster — represents one of the largest waves of foreign and domestic capital expenditure in India's infrastructure history, comparable in scale to major power or telecom build-outs of the past two decades.

👷

Jobs, skills and downstream industry

Gigawatt-scale campuses require construction labor, skilled electrical and cooling technicians, cybersecurity and network operations staff, and an ecosystem of ancillary suppliers (fiber, power equipment, modular construction). Telangana, Andhra Pradesh and Odisha are explicitly using data center incentives (stamp-duty waivers, GST reimbursement, dedicated power tariffs) to diversify their industrial base beyond traditional manufacturing.

☀️

Renewable energy as a forcing function

Because hyperscalers increasingly demand round-the-clock clean power, several of the largest projects — Reliance's Vizag cluster, Adani's integrated energy-compute platform — are bundling gigawatt-scale solar, wind and battery storage directly into the data center build. This is accelerating renewable capacity additions and grid-storage investment that India needs regardless of AI demand.

🚿

Better cooling economics emerging

Reliance's Jamnagar facility with Meta uses desalinated seawater instead of freshwater, and the broader industry trend toward closed-loop cooling (versus water-hungry evaporative systems) shows the sector responding, at least partially, to public pressure and cost incentives.

🚀

A broader digital-economy multiplier

Domestic AI compute lowers the cost and latency of building Indian-language models, government e-governance platforms (from Aadhaar-linked services to UPI-scale transaction processing), and startup products that would otherwise depend on renting capacity from data centers overseas. If even a fraction of the announced $150B+ pipeline converts into operating facilities on schedule, India would move from a minor player to a top-five global hub for AI infrastructure within this decade.

⚠️ Why This Build-Out Is a Risk for India, Right Now

Risks tied to the current, already-announced pipeline — distinct from the further-scaling risks covered later.

🚱

Water stress in already-scarce regions

With three-quarters of facilities sited in water-stressed districts, and cities like Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Chennai and Thane already running below daily municipal demand, large-scale industrial water withdrawal risks deepening shortages for residents, farmers and small businesses — populations that are disproportionately poorer and less able to absorb the cost of scarcity.

📜

Regulatory and disclosure gaps

India currently has no mandatory water-stress mapping and no standard thermal or environmental-impact assessment requirement specific to data centers — the sector is classified under IT/ITeS rules, exempting it from the environmental clearance process applied to other large infrastructure. Lawyers and activists have flagged the approved 1 GW Vizag Mega Data Centre Park and Google's facility there for failing to disclose water sourcing at the approval stage.

🔌

Grid strain and rising electricity costs

A roughly 6x increase in dedicated data center demand by 2030 will require substantial new generation, transmission and substation capacity concentrated in a handful of states. Without matching grid investment, local communities and industries risk higher tariffs or reduced reliability, echoing utility-rate disputes already seen around U.S. data centers.

🌦️

Weather-timing risk

India's data center expansion is accelerating just as forecasters flag a possible El Niño-driven weak 2026 monsoon — a combination that could sharply increase competition for water precisely when supply is under pressure, with little regulatory infrastructure in place to arbitrate between industrial and public use.

🏘️

Community friction is already visible

In Thane, the WakeUp Thanekars group is demanding a site-specific accounting of Amazon's Balkum facility after officials cited three contradictory water figures. Similar objections have emerged around the Vizag mega-cluster — early signals of the kind of local, town-by-town resistance the Brockovich Data Center project has documented extensively in the U.S.

⚖️

Equity and displacement dimensions

Researchers writing about the Vizag and Bengaluru cases note that communities most exposed to water withdrawal and land acquisition are often lower-income, Dalit, Adivasi and rural populations with the least influence over state-level investment decisions. Land acquisition of several hundred acres per campus raises resettlement, compensation and long-term local employment questions.

📈 If India Builds Even More Data Centers: The Cons

The current 12 GW pipeline is not the ceiling — analysts project India could reach 20-30+ GW beyond 2030 if AI demand keeps compounding. Here is what continued, larger-scale expansion specifically risks, on top of the current-pipeline concerns above.

🏭

Renewable buildout may not keep pace

If data center demand outgrows new renewable capacity additions, operators and grid operators will lean harder on coal and gas to fill the gap — especially during non-solar hours, since AI workloads run 24/7. That would quietly undo some of the climate gains from India's 50%+ non-fossil capacity milestone.

🌾

Direct competition with agriculture over water

Agriculture already uses ~80% of India's water. Even a small proportional increase in industrial water demand concentrated in specific irrigation-dependent basins could tip local water balances, particularly during weak-monsoon years, directly affecting farmer livelihoods and food security in those districts.

🏚️

Escalating land acquisition and displacement

Scaling from ~10 major hub sites today to dozens more implies proportionally larger and more frequent land acquisitions — likely pushing further into agricultural, coastal, and semi-forested land as the best-connected urban sites are exhausted, raising the scale of displacement and compensation disputes.

📉

Stranded-asset and overbuild risk

Much of the announced pipeline assumes AI compute demand keeps compounding at current rates. If global AI investment cools or shifts to more efficient models needing less raw compute, India could be left with expensive, underutilized gigawatt campuses, dedicated power plants, and desalination infrastructure built on now-outdated demand forecasts.

🌐

Deepening import dependence on chips & hardware

India has limited domestic capacity to manufacture the advanced GPUs and networking silicon these campuses require. A larger build-out means a larger recurring import bill and greater exposure to global chip-supply shocks and export-control policy shifts from chip-producing countries.

🧾

Regulatory scramble outpaced by construction speed

Facilities are being approved and built faster than water-stress mapping, environmental clearance rules, or disclosure standards are being written. Each additional gigawatt built before those rules exist makes retrofitting standards onto already-operating campuses harder and more expensive later.

🔮 Outcomes: Where This Is Headed by 2030

Two plausible trajectories, depending largely on whether disclosure rules, water governance and grid planning catch up with the pace of construction.

🟢 Best-case outcome

  • India reaches or exceeds its 12 GW 2030 data center target, largely matched with new dedicated renewable-plus-storage capacity rather than public grid draw.
  • Mandatory water-use disclosure and water-stress mapping become law, closing the ESG gaps documented across Nxtra, AdaniConneX, STT GDC, NTT, CtrlS and Sify.
  • Closed-loop and desalinated cooling become the default for new coastal and water-stressed builds, sharply cutting freshwater withdrawal per MW.
  • India becomes a top-five global AI infrastructure hub, with GPU capacity genuinely reserved for domestic startups and research, strengthening digital sovereignty.
  • Community reporting channels and environmental clearance requirements are extended to data centers, reducing disputes like Thane's before they escalate.

🔴 Worst-case outcome

  • Construction continues to outpace regulation; more Vizag- and Thane-style disputes emerge over undisclosed water sourcing, with no binding national framework to resolve them.
  • A weak monsoon year collides with peak construction, forcing acute local water-rationing decisions between data centers, farms and households in stressed basins.
  • Renewable additions lag AI power demand, pushing marginal generation back toward coal and eroding India's non-fossil capacity gains.
  • Some announced gigawatt-scale projects stall or scale back if global AI demand cools, leaving stranded land, power purchase agreements and half-built campuses.
  • Public trust erodes as "water-positive" and "carbon-neutral" claims from operators remain unverifiable, fueling the kind of community backlash already visible in the U.S. Brockovich dataset.

The realistic outcome likely lands somewhere between these two poles, and will vary sharply by state: expect Telangana and Tamil Nadu (with explicit policy frameworks) to trend toward the better-managed outcome faster than newer entrants like Odisha, where rules and precedent are still being written in real time.

📰 Community Impact & Local Reactions

Thane, Maharashtra — 2026

Residents challenge Amazon's water-use figures

WakeUp Thanekars, a local residents' group, publicly flagged three different official water-allocation numbers for the Balkum data center and demanded Amazon's environmental clearance documents in a city already running a daily water deficit. The dispute remains unresolved and has drawn attention from reporters covering similar issues in Vizag.

Visakhapatnam, Andhra Pradesh — 2026

Legal and civil-society pushback on clearances

Lawyers and environmental groups have called for the approved Vizag Mega Data Centre Park and Google's adjoining facility to disclose their water sourcing plan (groundwater, surface water or desalinated) before proceeding, arguing that treating data centers as generic "IT/ITeS" facilities understates their infrastructure-scale resource footprint.

Karnataka — ongoing

Bengaluru's water stress shapes siting decisions

Bengaluru's recurring municipal water shortages are cited by industry analysts as a reason hyperscale growth is increasingly steered toward Hyderabad, Vizag and coastal Andhra Pradesh rather than Karnataka, even though Bengaluru remains India's primary technology talent hub.

National — 2026

Calls for a binding disclosure framework

Multiple outlets (Eco-Business, Down To Earth, Scroll.in) have separately called for a national, enforceable framework requiring mandatory water-use disclosure, penalties for non-compliance, and water-equity assessments as part of environmental clearance — arguing that voluntary ESG reporting has proven inconsistent and unverifiable across the sector's largest operators.

📋 What Regulators & Operators Should Do Next

  • Mandate standardized, comparable, year-over-year water-withdrawal and water-consumption disclosure for every operator, closing the ESG reporting gaps documented across Nxtra, AdaniConneX, STT GDC, NTT, CtrlS and Sify.
  • Require water-stress mapping and environmental clearance for data centers above a defined capacity threshold, rather than exempting them under generic IT/ITeS classification.
  • Prioritize treated wastewater reuse and non-freshwater cooling (desalinated seawater, closed-loop, air-cooling) over groundwater extraction, especially for facilities in water-stressed districts.
  • Extend independent verification to "water-neutral" and "water-positive" corporate claims — current offsetting approaches do not address localized depletion in the specific district where a facility operates.
  • Align grid and transmission planning with the multi-gigawatt pipeline now publicly announced, so power costs and reliability for existing residential and industrial users are not degraded.
  • Set e-waste recycling and hardware lifecycle requirements specific to AI/GPU infrastructure, given its faster refresh cycle than traditional IT.
  • Give communities a formal channel — similar to the reporting mechanism this page links to — to flag site-specific concerns before and after a facility is approved.

🌍 India vs. the U.S.: A Comparative Snapshot

United States (per Brockovich Data Center Reporting, as of July 30, 2026)

  • 🟢 33 operational AI/hyperscale data centers tracked
  • 🟡 66 under construction
  • 🔴 47 proposed / in pipeline
  • 📍 8,837 community-reported concern locations nationwide

The U.S. project overlays its map with a live NOAA/NDMC drought monitor layer, since many proposed sites sit in drought-affected states — a direct parallel to India's water-stressed siting problem.

India (this tracker, August 2026)

  • 🟢 ~1.9 GW operational capacity across the hubs tracked here
  • 🟡 Multiple gigawatt-scale campuses under active construction (Hyderabad, Jamnagar, Pune)
  • 🔴 ~4.5 GW in the announced 5-year pipeline; ~12 GW projected by 2030
  • 📍 75% of facilities estimated to sit in water-stressed districts

India's build-out is smaller in absolute facility count today but is scaling faster in capacity terms — the jump from 0.78 GW (FY23) to a projected 12 GW (FY30) is roughly a 15x increase in seven years, a steeper curve than most Western markets are currently seeing.

The two countries share the same core tension: AI infrastructure investment is arriving faster than local regulatory frameworks can evaluate its water, power and land impact. In the U.S., that gap has shown up as community organizing, utility-rate disputes, and even electoral consequences for local officials perceived as too permissive. In India, it is showing up as unresolved RTI requests, contested clearance filings, and residents' groups demanding basic disclosure. Neither country yet has a fully mature, standardized public reporting system for data center resource use — which is exactly the gap both this project and Brockovich Data Center Reporting are trying to help close.
