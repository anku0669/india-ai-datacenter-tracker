India's AI Data Center Boom — Tracked Live

India is in the middle of the largest data-center build-out in its history. Hyperscalers, conglomerates and state governments have announced well over US $150 billion in new AI infrastructure since 2025 — from Reliance's 1.5 GW cluster in Visakhapatnam to Adani's US $100 billion, 2035 roadmap. This page tracks where that capacity is landing, how much power and water it consumes, and who bears the cost.

Inspired by and modeled after the U.S. community-awareness project Brockovich Data Center Reporting, which maps AI data centers and citizen concerns across the United States. This is an independent, India-focused adaptation built from public data — not affiliated with that project.

About This Tracker

This page is an independent, open-source effort to do for India what the Brockovich Data Center Reporting project has done for the United States: put the scattered, hard-to-find facts about AI infrastructure — who is building what, where, how big, and at what environmental cost — onto a single, continuously updatable page that any citizen, journalist, policymaker or investor can check. It is not affiliated with any government body, operator, or the original Brockovich project, and it does not represent itself as an official regulator's dataset.

Methodology: every figure on this page is sourced from public disclosures — company press releases, stock-exchange filings, sector reports from KPMG and SBI, state government incentive notifications, and investigative journalism from outlets including Down To Earth, Eco-Business, Scroll.in and The Hindu BusinessLine. Where sources disagree (which happens often, especially on water use), this page states the range and cites both figures rather than picking one. Every hub card and map marker lists its source inline. The dataset is maintained as plain, human-readable code (data.js) on GitHub, so anyone can audit, fork, or submit a correction.

🗓️ Timeline of Major Announcements (2025-2026)

Early 2025 — AWS commits $8.3 billion to expand its Mumbai region, adding to India's existing hyperscale base and signaling the start of an accelerated multi-year investment cycle.

December 2025 — AWS pledges an additional $7 billion for Hyderabad; Microsoft announces a $17.5 billion AI infrastructure investment spanning Hyderabad and Pune. Within weeks, the scale of committed hyperscaler capital in India roughly doubles.

Early-to-mid 2026 — AWS discloses a $430 million data center near Navi Mumbai (Taloja) and pledges a further $13 billion through 2030, bringing its cumulative India commitment to roughly $48 billion. Google announces a $15 billion, 1 GW campus in Visakhapatnam alongside three new subsea cable systems.

June 2026 — Reliance Industries and Meta announce a 168 MW, build-to-suit AI data center in Jamnagar, Gujarat — Meta's first dedicated facility in India, notable for its desalinated-seawater cooling design.

Mid-2026 — Reliance unveils its flagship 1.5 GW, three-phase Visakhapatnam cluster (~$17-19 billion), positioning it as potentially India's single largest data center investment, with Phase 1 targeted for commercial operation by October 2028.

August 2026 — Adani Group discloses plans for a $100 billion, decade-long (through 2035) roadmap to scale AdaniConneX from 2 GW to roughly 5 GW of national capacity, alongside a proposed ₹1 trillion, 1 GW facility in Odisha and continuing partnerships with Google and Microsoft. In the same window, community pushback surfaces publicly in Thane over Amazon's Balkum facility, and legal and civil-society groups intensify calls to suspend approvals for undisclosed water sourcing in the Vizag cluster.

⚡ Power Consumption: From 0.78 GW to a Projected 12 GW

India's installed data center capacity has grown from roughly 778 MW in FY23 to ~1.9 GW in FY26 — more than doubling in three years. Industry trackers now put the near-term pipeline at a further 4.5 GW over the next five years, with long-range projections from Adani and independent analysts pointing to ~12 GW by 2030, roughly a 6x increase from today.

For scale: a single 1 GW AI campus (like the ones planned by Reliance and Google in Visakhapatnam) draws roughly as much continuous power as a mid-sized Indian city. Multiply that across the more than a dozen gigawatt-scale projects now announced — Adani (2 GW → 5 GW), Reliance (1.5 GW Vizag + 168 MW Jamnagar), Google (1 GW), Microsoft ($17.5B across Hyderabad/Pune) and AWS ($48B cumulative across Mumbai/Hyderabad/ Navi Mumbai) — and the scale of new demand on India's grid becomes clear.

On efficiency, the picture is more positive: top Indian operators report Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) below 1.5, in line with global best practice, and roughly 50% of installed capacity is now paired with renewable power — solar, wind or captive renewable-plus-storage setups, as in Reliance's Vizag cluster and Adani's integrated energy-and-compute platform. Even so, 25-30% of existing capacity is considered under-specified for dense AI/GPU workloads and will need power and cooling retrofits.

About 90% of current capacity is concentrated in just four metros — Mumbai, Chennai, Delhi NCR and Bengaluru — putting concentrated new load on regional grids that were not originally sized for gigawatt-scale, 24/7 industrial compute demand. Hyderabad, Pune and Visakhapatnam are the fastest-growing "Tier II" hubs absorbing the overflow.

Unlike traditional industrial loads that fluctuate with shifts and seasons, AI training and inference clusters draw power around the clock at near-constant utilization, which is precisely why hyperscalers are pairing new campuses with dedicated generation rather than relying solely on the public grid. Reliance's Vizag cluster and Adani's platform both bundle captive solar, wind and battery storage directly into the data center build — in effect, India's AI boom is quietly becoming one of the country's largest private drivers of new renewable-plus-storage capacity, independent of any AI-specific policy mandate.

💧 Water Consumption: The Least-Disclosed Number

National data center water-use estimates. 2025 figure and 2030 projection per Eco-Business reporting; interim years interpolated to match the capacity growth curve.

Most Indian data centers still rely on evaporative cooling, which is cheap and energy-efficient but consumes large volumes of fresh water. One widely cited estimate from Karnataka puts consumption at roughly 25 million litres per MW per year. Applied to India's ~1.5-1.9 GW of live capacity, that alone implies tens of billions of litres a year before the current expansion wave is even counted.

Independent estimates suggest India's data centers drew about 150 billion litres of water in 2025, a figure projected to more than double to roughly 358 billion litres by 2030 as gigawatt-scale campuses come online. A single 100 MW facility using evaporative cooling can consume on the order of 2 million litres per day.

The geography is the real concern: an estimated 75% of India's data centers sit in water-stressed regions, and a WRI India mapping exercise found more than half of facilities in areas already competing for scarce municipal and agricultural water. Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Chennai — three of India's top data-center hubs — are also three cities that regularly face acute water shortages.

Nationally, data centers use a small share of India's total electricity (about 0.5% in 2025) and an even smaller share of total water withdrawal (roughly 0.02-0.03%) — but national averages hide the local reality. Withdrawing municipal or groundwater at industrial scale in a single water-stressed district can create real competition with residential and agricultural users, regardless of the national percentage.

Transparency is a persistent problem. A Down To Earth investigation of six major operators — Nxtra by Airtel, AdaniConneX, STT GDC India, NTT, CtrlS and Sify — found inconsistent, non-comparable ESG water disclosures with major year-to-year gaps: STT GDC reported total withdrawals rising to 1,149,020 KL in 2024; NTT disclosed 413,779 KL in 2023 but omitted water data in its 2025 report entirely; AdaniConneX has no standalone water disclosure at all. In Thane, residents found three contradictory official figures for the same Amazon facility — 10 KLD, 125 KLD and a planned 12 MLD municipal allocation — illustrating how hard it is for the public to verify real consumption.

Cooling technology choice matters enormously here. Evaporative and open-loop chilled-water systems lose most of their water to the atmosphere as vapor and cannot be meaningfully recycled on-site; closed-loop liquid cooling and air-based free cooling use far less water but typically cost more to build and, in hot climates, deliver a smaller efficiency benefit. Reliance's move to desalinated seawater at Jamnagar, and its planned desalination plant for the Vizag cluster, are among the first large-scale attempts in India to decouple gigawatt-scale AI cooling from freshwater and groundwater altogether — a model worth tracking as a possible national standard.

✅ Why This Build-Out Is Good for India

Digital sovereignty & strategic autonomy

Hosting AI compute domestically means Indian companies, startups and government agencies can run sensitive workloads — health records, financial data, defense-adjacent research — without depending entirely on foreign infrastructure. Adani's platform explicitly reserves GPU capacity for Indian startups, research institutions and deep-tech ventures, and frames the effort as part of a national "five-layer AI stack" (applications, models, chips, energy, data centers).

Direct and induced investment

The announced pipeline — Adani's $100B, AWS's cumulative $48B, Microsoft's $17.5B, Google's $15B, and Reliance's roughly $17B+ Vizag cluster — represents one of the largest waves of foreign and domestic capital expenditure in India's infrastructure history, comparable in scale to major power or telecom build-outs of the past two decades.

Jobs, skills and downstream industry

Gigawatt-scale campuses require construction labor, skilled electrical and cooling technicians, cybersecurity and network operations staff, and an ecosystem of ancillary suppliers (fiber, power equipment, modular construction). State governments in Telangana, Andhra Pradesh and Odisha are explicitly using data center incentives (stamp-duty waivers, GST reimbursement, dedicated power tariffs) to diversify their industrial base beyond traditional manufacturing.

Renewable energy as a forcing function

Because hyperscalers increasingly demand round-the-clock clean power, several of the largest projects — Reliance's Vizag cluster, Adani's integrated energy-compute platform — are bundling gigawatt-scale solar, wind and battery storage directly into the data center build. This is accelerating renewable capacity additions and grid-storage investment that India needs regardless of AI demand.

Better cooling economics are also emerging: Reliance's Jamnagar facility with Meta uses desalinated seawater instead of freshwater, and the broader industry trend toward closed-loop cooling (versus water-hungry evaporative systems) shows the sector responding, at least partially, to public pressure and cost incentives.

There is also a broader digital-economy multiplier effect. Domestic AI compute lowers the cost and latency of building Indian-language models, government e-governance platforms (from Aadhaar-linked services to UPI-scale transaction processing), and startup products that would otherwise depend on renting capacity from data centers overseas. Adani's platform explicitly frames its GPU reservation for Indian startups and research institutions as part of keeping AI value-creation — not just AI compute cost — inside the country. If even a fraction of the announced $150 billion-plus pipeline converts into operating facilities on schedule, India would move from a minor player to a top-five global hub for AI infrastructure within this decade.

⚠️ Why This Build-Out Is a Risk for India

Water stress in already-scarce regions

With three-quarters of facilities sited in water-stressed districts, and cities like Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Chennai and Thane already running below daily municipal demand, large-scale industrial water withdrawal risks deepening shortages for residents, farmers and small businesses — populations, researchers note, that are disproportionately poorer and less able to absorb the cost of scarcity.

Regulatory and disclosure gaps

India currently has no mandatory water-stress mapping and no standard thermal or environmental-impact assessment requirement specific to data centers — the sector is classified under IT/ITeS rules, which exempts it from the environmental clearance process applied to other large infrastructure. Lawyers and activists have specifically flagged the approved 1 GW Vizag Mega Data Centre Park and Google's facility there for failing to disclose their water sourcing (groundwater vs. surface water vs. desalinated) at the approval stage.

Grid strain and rising electricity costs

A roughly 6x increase in dedicated data center demand by 2030 will require substantial new generation, transmission and substation capacity concentrated in a handful of states. Without matching grid investment, local communities and industries risk higher tariffs or reduced reliability, echoing utility-rate disputes already seen around U.S. data centers.

Weather-timing risk

India's data center expansion is accelerating just as forecasters flag a possible El Niño-driven weak 2026 monsoon — a combination that could sharply increase competition for water precisely when supply is under pressure, with little regulatory infrastructure in place to arbitrate between industrial and public use.

Community-level friction has already surfaced: in Thane, the WakeUp Thanekars group is demanding a site-specific accounting of Amazon's Balkum facility after officials cited three contradictory water figures for the same site. Similar objections have emerged around the Vizag mega-cluster. These are early signals of the kind of local, town-by-town resistance that the Brockovich Data Center project has documented extensively in the United States — and a preview of what unmanaged growth could look like in India without better disclosure rules.

There are also equity dimensions specific to India. Researchers writing about the Vizag and Bengaluru cases note that the communities most exposed to water withdrawal and land acquisition for these campuses are often lower-income, Dalit, Adivasi and rural populations who have the least influence over state-level investment decisions and the least capacity to absorb a sudden loss of water access or agricultural land. Land acquisition itself — several hundred acres for a single hyperscale campus — raises additional questions about resettlement, compensation and long-term local employment that are separate from, but connected to, the energy and water debate.

📰 Community Impact & Local Reactions

Thane, Maharashtra — 2026

Residents challenge Amazon's water-use figures

WakeUp Thanekars, a local residents' group, publicly flagged three different official water-allocation numbers for the Balkum data center and demanded Amazon's environmental clearance documents in a city already running a daily water deficit. The dispute remains unresolved and has drawn attention from reporters covering similar issues in Vizag.

Visakhapatnam, Andhra Pradesh — 2026

Legal and civil-society pushback on clearances

Lawyers and environmental groups have called for the approved Vizag Mega Data Centre Park and Google's adjoining facility to disclose their water sourcing plan (groundwater, surface water or desalinated) before proceeding, arguing that treating data centers as generic "IT/ITeS" facilities understates their infrastructure-scale resource footprint.

Karnataka — ongoing

Bengaluru's water stress shapes siting decisions

Bengaluru's recurring municipal water shortages are cited by industry analysts as a reason hyperscale growth is increasingly steered toward Hyderabad, Vizag and coastal Andhra Pradesh rather than Karnataka, even though Bengaluru remains India's primary technology talent hub.

National — 2026

Calls for a binding disclosure framework

Multiple outlets (Eco-Business, Down To Earth, Scroll.in) have separately called for a national, enforceable framework requiring mandatory water-use disclosure, penalties for non-compliance, and water-equity assessments as part of environmental clearance — arguing that voluntary ESG reporting has proven inconsistent and unverifiable across the sector's largest operators.

📋 What Regulators & Operators Should Do Next

  • Mandate standardized, comparable, year-over-year water-withdrawal and water-consumption disclosure for every operator, closing the ESG reporting gaps documented across Nxtra, AdaniConneX, STT GDC, NTT, CtrlS and Sify.
  • Require water-stress mapping and environmental clearance for data centers above a defined capacity threshold, rather than exempting them under generic IT/ITeS classification.
  • Prioritize treated wastewater reuse and non-freshwater cooling (desalinated seawater, closed-loop, air-cooling) over groundwater extraction, especially for facilities in water-stressed districts.
  • Extend independent verification to "water-neutral" and "water-positive" corporate claims — current offsetting approaches do not address localized depletion in the specific district where a facility operates.
  • Align grid and transmission planning with the multi-gigawatt pipeline now publicly announced, so power costs and reliability for existing residential and industrial users are not degraded.
  • Give communities a formal channel — similar to the reporting mechanism this page links to — to flag site-specific concerns before and after a facility is approved.

🌍 India vs. the U.S.: A Comparative Snapshot

United States (per Brockovich Data Center Reporting, as of July 30, 2026)

  • 🟢 33 operational AI/hyperscale data centers tracked
  • 🟡 66 under construction
  • 🔴 47 proposed / in pipeline
  • 📍 8,837 community-reported concern locations nationwide

The U.S. project overlays its map with a live NOAA/NDMC drought monitor layer, since many proposed sites sit in drought-affected states — a direct parallel to India's water-stressed siting problem.

India (this tracker, August 2026)

  • 🟢 ~1.9 GW operational capacity across the hubs tracked here
  • 🟡 Multiple gigawatt-scale campuses under active construction (Hyderabad, Jamnagar, Pune)
  • 🔴 ~4.5 GW in the announced 5-year pipeline; ~12 GW projected by 2030
  • 📍 75% of facilities estimated to sit in water-stressed districts

India's build-out is smaller in absolute facility count today but is scaling faster in capacity terms — the jump from 0.78 GW (FY23) to a projected 12 GW (FY30) is roughly a 15x increase in seven years, a steeper curve than most Western markets are currently seeing.

The two countries share the same core tension: AI infrastructure investment is arriving faster than local regulatory frameworks can evaluate its water, power and land impact. In the U.S., that gap has shown up as community organizing, utility-rate disputes, and even electoral consequences for local officials perceived as too permissive. In India, it is showing up as unresolved RTI requests, contested clearance filings, and residents' groups demanding basic disclosure. Neither country yet has a fully mature, standardized public reporting system for data center resource use — which is exactly the gap both this project and Brockovich Data Center Reporting are trying to help close.
